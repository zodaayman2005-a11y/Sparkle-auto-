CORE MODULE 02 — ART DIRECTION INTELLIGENCE

Art direction intelligence → original web visual direction

Start from the project, not from a picture

This skill must work even when the user supplies no visual examples at all.

Derive the visual direction from the actual project inputs:

• brand rules and design tokens,
• product truth and real screenshots,
• audience and business goal,
• page narrative and conversion goal,
• tone of voice and language direction,
• existing approved assets,
• technical constraints,
• accessibility and performance requirements.

Do not ask for example images as a default prerequisite. They are optional inspiration only when the user chooses to provide them; the skill must remain fully operational without them.

Build a visual thesis from evidence

Translate the project into a short visual thesis before generating assets.

Write:

```text
Audience / decision:
Business goal:
Human truth:
One message:
Visual verb:
Primary metaphor:
Rendering family:
Dominant geometry:
Material / lighting logic:
Graphic palette roles:
Product proof that must remain real:
What is deliberately absent:
Mobile implication:
```

A direction is not a list of adjectives. It must change real decisions about composition, scale, subject behavior, spacing, prop choice, layer structure, and integration.

Research only when it can change a decision

Use research when the brief is open, a category convention is unknown, or a current technical fact is needed.
Do not browse galleries simply to collect attractive images.

If external inspiration is studied, transfer principles rather than copying protected expression:

• composition principle,
• rhythm,
• material logic,
• interaction pattern,
• information hierarchy,
• production technique.

Do not copy another brand’s logo, character, proprietary screenshot, distinctive illustration, or page composition one-for-one.

The final result should be specific to the current project’s brand and message.

Turn adjectives into decisions

“Simple” becomes one dominant subject/action, one primary metaphor, limited props,
clear silhouette, and no nonessential labels.

“Premium” becomes controlled materials, credible contact/light, deliberate spacing,
careful edges, legible typography, and a composition that survives a small screen.

“Creative” becomes an unexpected but understandable relationship between objects,
not a longer list of decorative items.

“Visually rich” becomes stronger focal moments, varied section rhythm, an ownable
image family, and useful detail where it supports the message—not image count.

“Playful” becomes controlled exaggeration, timing, or geometry—not random stickers.

“Trustworthy” becomes product truth, restrained claims, stable hierarchy, and credible detail.

A concept must contain a visual verb

Weak: a friendly business owner next to a dashboard.

Stronger: an oversized telephone pulls the owner away from the work they need to manage.
The verb is “pulls”; the pain is operational dependence; the prop creates the scale contrast.

Other useful verbs include:
aligns, unfolds, reveals, balances, connects, compresses, releases, clears, stacks,
separates, routes, catches, unlocks, stabilizes, guides, lifts, filters, organizes.

Do not force physical comedy into a brand that calls for restraint.

For each major story moment, write:

```text
Human truth:
One message:
Visual verb:
Primary metaphor:
Why this brand could own it:
What real proof appears nearby:
What is deliberately absent:
```

Direction selection

For an open brief, create up to three meaningfully different territories.
They must differ in narrative, geometry, subject relationship, depth/material, and rhythm.
Do not present the same hero in three colorways.

Choose using:

1. clarity in one second,
2. brand fit,
3. product truth compatibility,
4. mobile resilience,
5. production feasibility,
6. performance cost,
7. accessibility,
8. distinctiveness.

For a locked brand, explore shot/concept variations without redesigning the identity system.
Existing brand decisions are constraints, not invitations to reinvent the whole visual language.

Proceed under delegated autonomy when the direction is clear.
Escalate only material identity changes, product claims, added external spend, or other decisions that truly require approval.

Family grammar

Document the visual family before batch production:

• Rendering family: photography / stylized 3D / flat illustration / collage / typography-led / mixed.
• Camera: height, angle, perspective strength, horizon, close-up versus full body.
• Light: dominant direction, softness, fill, contact shadow, consistency across assets.
• Materials: small bounded set; roughness/finish described concretely.
• Shape: geometry, edge softness, density, repeated motif.
• Palette: graphic colors versus natural subject/material colors; no uncontrolled accents.
• Scale: one dominant object, secondary scale, allowable exaggeration.
• Integration: transparent cutout, opaque scene, full-bleed plate, or real image frame.
• Typography relationship: where copy lives and how the artwork protects it.
• Motion potential: what can move meaningfully and what must stay static.
• Refusals: specific attractive choices that do not belong in this world.

Identity without user-supplied imagery

When the project contains a recurring generated character or object but no approved visual asset exists:

1. Write a locked identity specification in text.
2. Generate one calibration asset only.
3. Inspect face/form, proportions, outfit/materials, lighting, camera, and brand fit.
4. Correct that asset until it is acceptable.
5. Mark the accepted result as the internal identity anchor for this project.
6. Use that anchor consistently for later generations when the tool supports image-conditioned editing or generation.
7. When the tool cannot use an anchor, reduce variation by keeping the same descriptive block, camera family, outfit/materials, and controlled scene complexity.
8. Never claim perfect identity consistency when the tool cannot guarantee it.

The user does not need to arrive with a picture for this workflow to work.

Editorial rhythm

Map the page at thumbnail scale.
Alternate open and dense moments.
Use contrast in layout family, type scale, proof size, and visual intensity rather than wallpapering
all sections with the same texture, character pose, or device treatment.

No more than two consecutive sections with the same family by default.
An image-free pricing table or text-led FAQ can be the correct pause between story scenes.

Visual richness budget

A visually rich site is not a site where everything is illustrated.

For each viewport, deliberately choose:

• one dominant focal element,
• zero to two supporting graphic accents,
• real product proof when relevant,
• sufficient empty space,
• a clear copy zone.

Use the strongest custom art only where it changes understanding, emotion, or memorability.

Three fast tests

One-second meaning: can a viewer name the point, not just the objects?

Subtraction: remove one decorative item. If the message improves, keep it removed.

Brand without logo: does the world retain the brand’s specific character without
placing the same mascot, color blob, or decorative motif in every gap?

These are design heuristics, not measured user-study claims.
